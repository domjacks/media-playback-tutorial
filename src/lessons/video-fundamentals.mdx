## Pixels And Raster Images

Digital video is a sequence of raster images shown over time. A raster image is a grid of picture elements, or pixels. Each pixel stores values that describe the colour and brightness at one point in the picture.

A single video frame is one raster image. Playback works by presenting many frames on a timeline quickly enough that the viewer perceives motion.

- Pixels are spatial samples of a picture.
- Frames are raster images placed at times on the media timeline.
- Video quality starts with how many samples exist and how accurately each sample describes light.

## Resolution And Aspect Ratio

Resolution is the number of pixels in each frame. A 1920 by 1080 video has 2,073,600 luma sample positions per frame, while a 1280 by 720 video has 921,600. More samples can preserve more spatial detail, but they also increase decode work and usually need more bitrate.

Aspect ratio is the shape of the picture, such as 16:9 or 4:3. Player layout should respect the encoded display shape so the image is not stretched, squeezed, or cropped by accident.

- Resolution affects sharpness, decode cost, and bandwidth.
- Display size and device pixel density decide whether extra resolution is visible.
- Aspect ratio belongs to presentation; it is not the same thing as file size or bitrate.

## RGB And YCbCr

Screens often display colour as RGB: red, green, and blue light values. Video systems commonly store colour as YCbCr instead. Y carries luma, which is brightness detail. Cb and Cr carry chroma difference information, which describes colour relative to luma.

YCbCr is useful because human vision is more sensitive to brightness detail than colour detail. Video can keep full luma resolution while storing less chroma detail, saving data with less visible damage than reducing every component equally.

- RGB is convenient for displays and canvas-style pixel work.
- YCbCr is common in encoded video and broadcast workflows.
- Colour conversion and metadata help the browser map encoded values to the display.

## Bit Depth

Bit depth is the number of bits used for each component sample. 8-bit video gives each component 256 possible values. 10-bit video gives each component 1,024 possible values, which allows smoother gradients and gives HDR workflows more precision.

Higher bit depth increases raw data size before compression. A 10-bit stream is not automatically better than an 8-bit stream, but it gives the codec and display pipeline more precision to work with.

- 8-bit SDR video is common and broadly compatible.
- 10-bit video is common for HDR and higher-quality distribution.
- Browser support depends on codec, profile, operating system, GPU, and display.

## Chroma Subsampling

Chroma subsampling stores colour at a lower resolution than luma. The notation describes how chroma samples are shared across nearby pixels. 4:4:4 keeps full chroma detail. 4:2:2 halves chroma horizontally. 4:2:0 halves chroma horizontally and vertically across a two-line area.

Most web video is 4:2:0 because it compresses well and usually looks good for natural images. Text, graphics, and sharp colour edges can suffer more because their chroma detail matters.

- 4:4:4 keeps one Cb and one Cr sample for every luma sample.
- 4:2:2 keeps full luma but shares chroma across pairs of horizontal samples.
- 4:2:0 keeps full luma but shares chroma across a small block of neighbouring samples.

## Frame Rate And Scan Type

Frame rate is how many pictures are shown per second. Common values include 24 fps for film-like motion, 25 or 30 fps for broadcast and web video, and 50 or 60 fps for sport, games, and very smooth motion.

Progressive video stores each frame as a complete picture. Interlaced video stores each picture as alternating fields, traditionally one field for odd lines and one for even lines. Interlacing helped older broadcast systems reduce bandwidth, but modern web playback usually prefers progressive video.

- A 60 fps stream has twice as many frame times as a 30 fps stream.
- The media timeline is continuous even though video frames are discrete.
- Interlaced sources usually need deinterlacing before clean progressive display.

## Colour And Brightness

Colour gamut describes the range of colours a video can represent. Dynamic range describes the span between the darkest and brightest picture detail. Both are properties of how numbers in the stream are interpreted, not of the numbers themselves, so a stream needs signalling that says how to read them.

Three code points, defined in ITU-T H.273 (also published as ISO/IEC 23091-2, "CICP"), describe the interpretation. Players and containers pass them around as three small integers.

- Colour primaries say which red, green and blue chromaticities, and which white point, the signal refers to. 1 is BT.709 (the HD standard, close to sRGB primaries), 9 is BT.2020 (the wide gamut used for UHD and HDR), 12 is Display P3 with a D65 white point.
- Transfer characteristics say how stored values relate to light. 1 is BT.709, 13 is sRGB, 16 is PQ (SMPTE ST 2084), 18 is HLG (ARIB STD-B67).
- Matrix coefficients say how to convert between YCbCr and RGB. 1 is BT.709, 5 and 6 are BT.601, 9 is BT.2020 non-constant luminance, and 0 is identity (the data is already RGB).
- The value 2 means unspecified in all three tables.

## Transfer Functions And HDR

A transfer function is a curve applied to light before quantising, so that limited code values are spent where the eye notices steps. SDR curves such as BT.709 and sRGB are gamma-like and are defined relative to a display of roughly 100 nits.

PQ is absolute. Each code value maps to a fixed luminance, up to 10,000 nits, so a display must tone-map anything brighter than it can show. HLG is relative and scene-referred: the lower part of the curve resembles a conventional gamma and the top compresses highlights, so an HLG signal degrades tolerably on an SDR display. That backwards compatibility is the main reason broadcasters use it.

- HDR10 is 10-bit, BT.2020 primaries, PQ, plus static metadata.
- HLG normally pairs BT.2020 primaries with the ARIB STD-B67 curve and needs no metadata.
- HDR needs at least 10 bits per component to avoid visible banding across the wider luminance range.

Static HDR metadata helps the display tone-map. SMPTE ST 2086 describes the mastering display: its primaries, white point, and maximum and minimum luminance. MaxCLL (maximum content light level) and MaxFALL (maximum frame-average light level), from CTA-861.3, give the brightest pixel and brightest frame average in the content, in nits.

## Matrix Coefficients And YCbCr

YCbCr is derived from non-linear R'G'B' using two constants, `Kr` and `Kb`. Luma is `Y' = Kr R' + (1 - Kr - Kb) G' + Kb B'`, and Cb and Cr are scaled differences B' - Y' and R' - Y'.

- BT.601 uses `Kr = 0.299` and `Kb = 0.114`.
- BT.709 uses `Kr = 0.2126` and `Kb = 0.0722`.
- BT.2020 non-constant luminance uses `Kr = 0.2627` and `Kb = 0.0593`.

Decoding with the wrong matrix does not break playback. It shifts hue and saturation slightly, mostly in reds and greens, which makes it easy to ship unnoticed.

## Limited And Full Range

Broadcast-style video uses limited range, also called TV or studio range. For 8-bit it places luma black at 16 and white at 235, and chroma between 16 and 240, leaving headroom and footroom for filtering overshoot. At 10 bits the equivalent values are 64 to 940 for luma and 64 to 960 for chroma. Full range, also called PC or JPEG range, uses the whole 0 to 255 (or 0 to 1023).

Web video is almost always limited range. The flag that says which is in use is `video_full_range_flag`, and a stream with the flag missing is normally treated as limited.

## Where It Is Signalled

The same four values can appear in several places, and a robust player or packager makes them agree.

- H.264 and HEVC carry them in the VUI inside the SPS: `video_signal_type_present_flag`, `video_full_range_flag`, `colour_primaries`, `transfer_characteristics` and `matrix_coeffs`. HDR metadata travels as SEI messages for mastering display colour volume and content light level.
- MP4 carries them in the `colr` box inside the sample entry. A `colr` with colour type `nclx` holds `colour_primaries`, `transfer_characteristics`, `matrix_coefficients` and `full_range_flag`. HDR metadata uses the `mdcv` and `clli` boxes.
- Codec strings for VP9 and AV1 embed them. VP9 ends with chroma, colour primaries, transfer, matrix and full-range fields, and AV1 does the same. HEVC and H.264 strings do not, so the codec string alone cannot tell you whether an HEVC stream is SDR or HDR10.
- DASH can add `SupplementalProperty` or `EssentialProperty` descriptors with the same code points, so that a player can filter Representations before downloading anything.

## What Breaks When It Is Wrong

Colour metadata errors rarely stop playback. They change the picture, which makes them harder to diagnose than a decode failure.

- Limited-range video treated as full range looks washed out: black at code 16 is displayed as dark grey and white at 235 never reaches full brightness.
- Full-range video treated as limited range crushes shadows and clips highlights, because values outside 16 to 235 are cut off.
- A BT.709 stream decoded with a BT.601 matrix, or the reverse, gives subtle hue shifts in skin tones and foliage.
- BT.2020 content shown as if it were BT.709 looks desaturated.
- PQ content shown as if it were SDR looks dim and flat, and HDR10 without correct tone mapping loses highlight detail.

When two renditions look different on the same screen, compare their `colr` and VUI values before you blame the encoder.

## Checking HDR Support In The Browser

A page can ask whether the display is HDR capable with a media query, and whether the decoder path supports a specific stream with Media Capabilities. The media query describes the display, and Media Capabilities describes decode and, with `powerEfficient` and `smooth`, how well it will run.

<Snippet title="Ask The Browser About HDR" explain="matchMedia reports display capability. decodingInfo accepts colorGamut (srgb, p3, rec2020), transferFunction (srgb, pq, hlg) and hdrMetadataType (smpteSt2086, smpteSt2094-10, smpteSt2094-40). Support is uneven across browsers, so treat a missing or false answer as 'not known to work'.">

```js
const hdrDisplay = matchMedia("(dynamic-range: high)").matches;
const wideGamut = matchMedia("(color-gamut: p3)").matches;

const info = await navigator.mediaCapabilities.decodingInfo({
  type: "media-source",
  video: {
    contentType: 'video/mp4; codecs="hvc1.2.4.L153.90"',
    width: 3840,
    height: 2160,
    bitrate: 15_000_000,
    framerate: 24,
    colorGamut: "rec2020",
    transferFunction: "pq",
    hdrMetadataType: "smpteSt2086",
  },
});

console.log({ hdrDisplay, wideGamut }, info.supported, info.smooth, info.powerEfficient);
```

</Snippet>

- A player should surface compatibility failures clearly instead of pretending every rendition is equivalent.
- Offer an SDR fallback Representation, because an HDR-capable decoder on an SDR display still needs tone mapping that not every platform does well.

<Question prompt="An 8-bit limited-range video is displayed by a player that wrongly treats it as full range. What do black and white look like, and what flag would normally tell the player which range applies?">

Limited range puts black at code 16 and white at 235. Read as full range, black is 16 / 255, about 6% grey instead of true black, and white is 235 / 255, about 92% brightness, so the picture looks washed out. The stream signals the range with `video_full_range_flag` in the H.264 or HEVC VUI (or `full_range_flag` in the MP4 `colr` box); when it is missing the stream is normally treated as limited.

</Question>
